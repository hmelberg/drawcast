(a) In 1776 Adam Smith posed a puzzle. Nothing is more useful than water, yet it buys almost nothing.
(b) And diamonds are no use at all, but cost a fortune. Is that a puzzle? Diamonds are pretty.
[ASK] Why is water cheap and diamonds dear?
  [right] Price follows the last glass, not the first: we have so much water that one more is worth very little.
(b) Hang on. What do you mean, the last glass?
(a) Picture your glasses of water in a day. The first one keeps you alive.
(a) The next ones go on tea, on washing, on the plants. Each is worth a little less than the one before.
(b) So I keep going until one more glass isn't worth what it costs.
(a) Exactly. And water is so plentiful that its price is tiny, so you go far out, to where one more glass is worth next to nothing.
(a) Diamonds are so scarce that even the next one is still way up here.
(a) Economists call it marginal utility. It took them a century after Smith to work it out, in the 1870s.

(a) Gladly. In the desert, the last glass is the first.
