For years, people said seven folds was the limit.
Then, in 2002, a high-school student in California, Britney Gallivan, set out to prove them wrong.
She worked out how much paper each fold would need, and found a roll of toilet paper about one point two kilometres long.
Then she folded it in half twelve times, in the same direction.
Nine years later, students from Saint Mark's School taped toilet paper down a corridor at MIT and managed thirteen.
Why is it so hard? Each fold doubles the layers: one, two, four, eight, sixteen, thirty-two.
After twelve folds, there are four thousand and ninety-six.
And each fold needs far more length than the last, because the paper must wrap around an ever thicker stack.
Keep doubling a sheet a tenth of a millimetre thick, forty-two times, and the stack would reach past the Moon.
