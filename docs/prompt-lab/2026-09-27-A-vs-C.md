## Reading (mine; Hans's blind verdicts are what counts)

The same pattern shows in all five cases:

- **C explains better.** The treatment writer, free of the JSON, goes further
  into the mechanism and the concrete case:
  - **Money:** an island with 100 coins and 100 loaves, then the two real US
    episodes (2008–14 against 2020–22), with velocity as the "money that
    moves".
  - **Antibiotics:** a billion cells, a mutant that was already present,
    Luria–Delbrück, "a billion has become one".
  - **Sum 1 + … + n:** dots paired row by row, 1+5 = 2+4 = 3+3 = 6.
  - **Fridge:** a boiling-point-against-pressure graph, which is the actual
    trick.
  - **Mountain:** the lapse rate, the actual numbers, and a winter inversion.

  A's explanations are correct and tidy, but more generic: in case 1, an
  AD/AS chart with the textbook story.
- **A looks better.** A draws one big figure that fills the page. C's pages
  are crowded and small: little cards, jars, graphs and readouts spread
  around, small text, and more overlap warnings (C 10 against A 3). There are
  also garbled measure labels ("≈ 2 km260", "5Σ30").
- **Why:** the treatment's FIGURE section places things in words ("upper
  left: …", "bottom strip: …", "right margin kept empty until beat 13"), and it
  plans many pieces. The staging step obeys. In A the same model decides
  placement itself, while it looks at the layout tools (`fit`, group layouts,
  `box`), and keeps to fewer, larger things.
- The staging step kept the treatment's lines almost word for word, which is
  what it was asked to do.

## What this suggests

1. The split works for the **explanation**. It breaks the **layout**, because
   the wrong step decides where things go.
2. **Next arm, C2 (cheap to test):** the treatment says *what* the figure is
   and what changes, but not *where*. It plans one main figure, drawn large,
   plus at most one or two supporting pieces. The staging step owns the page,
   and may merge or drop a supporting piece that crowds it.
3. **Then D:** C2 plus the look pass. A critic sees these same contact sheets
   and would catch every layout problem listed above.
4. **Drop the pedagogy pass** in any case: five runs out of five, no effect.

Reading:

- **Better pages than C, still not as clean as A.** The main figure is now
  large in four of the five cases: the fridge fills the page, the mountain
  and its air parcel are big, the staircase block is big. The explanations
  keep C's depth.
- **Case 1 (money) stays busy.** It has a circular flow of coins, readouts,
  cards and a formula. The topic invites many pieces, and "at most two
  supporting pieces" did not hold.
- **Remaining faults are small but visible:** labels on strokes; the heading
  crowded by a card in the Norwegian case; small text in scratch cards. These
  are exactly what a critic looking at the page would catch.
- **The staging step works harder.** Four of the five runs needed repair
  rounds, against none in A or C, and one ends with a lint error. It is also
  the slowest arm (~7.4 min) and costs the same as A.

## Where this leaves the experiment (my recommendation)

- The two-step split gives **better explanations**. I'd keep it, as the
  single-figure path's "treatment" step, when this is taken further.
- **Layout needs eyes, not more words.** Neither arm C nor C2's wording fixes
  the page reliably. The next arm to build is **D = C2 + the look pass**: one
  vision call over these same contact sheets. It returns a short list of
  concrete page problems (overlaps, small text, crowding, a supporting piece
  to drop), and one staging round applies them.
- **Drop the pedagogy pass:** in five runs out of five it had no effect.
- The treatment adds 2–4 minutes. If that matters, the treatment call could
  run at medium effort. That is worth one run to measure.

Before building D, Hans's blind verdicts on the two compare pages should
decide whether the explanation gain is real to him, since that is the point
of the split.

## Run 1c: arm D, the look pass (run by agents, not the API)

D = C2 plus one look: a critic sees the rendered frames (one per spoken
line) and lists at most 8 page problems (`src/llm/prompts/look-v1.md`), then
one fix of the spec. To test the idea before paying for API runs, each of the
five C2 specs was given to an agent in Claude Code (Opus 5.5). It wrote the
critique from the pictures alone (`*-D-critique.md`), fixed the spec
(`*-D.json`), and rendered it (`*-D.jpg`). That is a different model and
setting from the app's call (see "Caveat"). Blind pages: `compare-C2-D.html`
and `compare-A-D.html`.

| Case | Agent's verdict vs C2 | Mine (checked 2 of 5 by eye) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 econ | about the same: collisions, a sync bug and a wrong number fixed, but the enlarged figure spilled over and the view zoomed out, so everything got smaller | agree |
| 2 medicine | better: large cavity, readable key, countable dots, every sweep reads | agree — clearly cleaner |
| 3 math | better, figure still only about half the page | — |
| 4 thing (fridge) | better: closed loop, labels by their parts, all lint warnings gone | — |
| 5 norsk | better: readable distance card, parcel matches its line, temperature line tied to the axis | — |

What we learned:

- **The critic sees real problems.** Its lists are concrete, and most of them
  are things no lint rule catches: a line that doesn't match the picture ($5
  labelled 9.1 %), a number that contradicts the narration (15 red dots for
  "ten"), a snap-back under the wrong line, an empty half page.
- **One fix round is not always enough.** A fix can overshoot (econ: bigger
  became off-page, so the view shrank). **A second look** after the fix would
  catch that. In the app that would be look, fix, look, and stop when the
  critic answers NONE, with at most two rounds.
- **Still frames can't show emphasis.** Highlights, focus and flowing dots
  never appear in a frame, so the critic can't judge them. Frames taken
  mid-gesture would fix that; it's a small change to the harness.
- **The WISH lines agree with each other.** Three of five asked for the same
  kind of thing: a **population / dot-field element** with counts ("turn 10
  red", "divide until full"); a **dot grid driven by n**; a **label that rides
  a moving thing** and updates its value (the rising air parcel, the fridge's
  refrigerant). These are candidates for new structure-derived commands.

Caveat: an agent can look again while it works and iterate on validity; the
app's call cannot. So D here is an upper bound on what one API look + fix
round will do. Confirm with 2–3 real API runs before building it into the app.
